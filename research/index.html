01

Fully passive flapping-foil turbines

2021–2025. Doctoral work, continued at KIOST.

A flapping-foil turbine extracts power from a current by letting a hydrofoil heave and pitch instead of spin. Making that motion fully passive — no motor imposing the pitch, no controller — is what makes the device simple enough to be worth building, and also what makes it hard to study. The operating point is not something you set; it emerges from the coupling between the foil, the springs, the linkage and the flow, so every design question has to be answered by measurement.

I designed and ran four campaigns on this device, each answering one of those questions, in circulating water channels from laboratory scale up to the large facility at KIOST.

Four campaigns

2023
Renewable Energy

Chains linking the pitch and arm motion of the two foils let the pair start and run with no actuator, settling into a near-90° phase difference that removes the zero-power zones from the combined power curve.

DOI
2024
Renewable Energy

A crank-rocker mechanism turns the flapping of both foils into rotation of a single shaft and transmits 92.1% of the power it receives, against a theoretical ceiling of 95.3%; what is lost goes to short negative-torque spells as the rear foil passes through the wake of the front one.

DOI
2024
Energy Sci. & Eng.

Ten degrees of yaw misalignment costs about 10% of the power and adds about 30% to the system load, and that added load acts perpendicular to the flow and swings periodically with the flapping cycle.

DOI
2026
Int. J. Energy Res.

Elliptical foils in tandem let the turbine start in a current 49% slower than a NACA-section turbine needs and run with the flow coming from either direction, for 12.5–13.5% less efficiency.

02

Biomimetic caudal-fin propulsion

2025–present. Ongoing at KIOST.

A fish does not push water backwards with a propeller; it bends. Turning that into a propulsion system for an underwater vehicle means reproducing enough of the tail's motion to get useful thrust, and then finding out what the rest of the vehicle has to absorb in return.

I designed an apparatus that lets a caudal fin move in several degrees of freedom inside a circulating water channel while thrust, side force and yaw moment are recorded together. The part that interests me most is the recoil: a body-and-caudal-fin stroke is asymmetric in time, so the hull experiences a periodic yaw disturbance that scales with the thrust you are trying to produce. Characterising that at close to operational scale — not at the small-model scale where most of the published work sits — is what the current campaign is for.

Three parts of this work were presented at the 78th Annual Meeting of the APS Division of Fluid Dynamics in 2025. A manuscript on the near-operational-scale propulsion and yaw recoil characterisation is in preparation.

03

Test and evaluation

2025–2026. Marine unmanned systems.

Two laboratories can measure the thrust of the same thruster and disagree, and usually the disagreement is procedural rather than physical — a different mounting stiffness, a different settling time, a different definition of the averaging window. That is tolerable inside one group and useless the moment a number is supposed to support a comparison between vehicles.

Within the KIOST programme on integrated performance test and evaluation for marine unmanned systems, I work on the propulsion side: building the test environment, writing the measurement procedure, and checking that it holds up — repeatability across runs, traceability from raw signal to reported figure, and an explicit uncertainty statement rather than an implied one. Part of the output is draft group standards, which forces the procedure to be specified tightly enough that someone else can execute it.
